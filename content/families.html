Family density

Whether a small category is a gap worth filling or a domain that is simply finished.

The obvious reading of a family table is that thin means missing. It is wrong more often than it is right, because it conflates small with under-served. What separates a healthy small family from a real gap is not the count but the spread: how far apart the best and worst member score. A family of four with a twelve-point spread is being measured, and each member is pulling its weight. A family of sixty with a spread of zero is not being measured at all.

Most skills land in Unclassified on a fresh install, because config/taxonomy.json ships as a starter with empty member lists rather than a copy of somebody else's inventory. Run atlas doctor to list them, add the slugs under a family, and re-run. A family that is small because you have not classified anything yet is the one reading in this table that is definitely wrong.

Small and finished looks exactly like small and missing

Some domains are genuinely bounded. A family covering one host application's plugins can only grow when a new host plugin does; a family covering document formats is capped by how many formats matter. These read as gaps in a count-only view, and topping them up would add listing weight and no capability. The tell is a tight grade distribution — a small family whose members are all at the top of the scale is a finished domain, because there is nothing left to fix in it.

The converse is worth stating too, because it is the more common failure: a domain that is bounded but under-filled looks identical in a count column, and the only way to tell them apart is to ask what the family would have to contain if it were complete. That question needs knowledge of your work, which is why this tool stops at size and spread and leaves the verdict to you.

A rubric that returns a constant has stopped ranking

This is the finding worth more than any individual score in the table, and it is about the measurement rather than the library. When a large family scores identically across every member — same total, same parts, spread of zero — the cause is usually that most dimensions saturate for that shape of skill: a long description cannot get better, a healthy body cannot get better, and a directory-structure dimension sits at its floor because every member has the same layout. Five of six dimensions return their maximum, the sixth returns its minimum, and every member lands on the same number.

That is not a signal wearing the costume of noise; it is the reverse. A constant over your largest family is a blind spot precisely where you have the most to lose from not knowing which member is better. atlas doctor reports any family of ten or more with zero score variance as a notice, and deliberately as a notice only: re-tuning the weights would change every score in the report and break comparability with the stored baselines that atlas diff measures against. The threshold is ten because a family of one has a spread of zero by arithmetic, not by blindness.

What would actually fix it, when comparability is worth spending: the dimension that would separate such a family is usually not a re-weighting of an existing one but a new axis the rubric has no way to express — for a family of visual systems, whether a member ships something executable rather than prose alone. That belongs in a deliberate rubric v2 with a fresh baseline, not in a quiet edit to config/rubric.json.

A low body score is ambiguous, and the rubric reads it one way

The body band is a curve, not a floor: thin earns less than good, and very long earns less again. That is the right shape — a single-file document of forty thousand characters is a real cost — but it makes a low score ambiguous between too short and too long, and only one of those is a defect. A skill whose SKILL.md is already a lean index delegating to a references/ bundle is doing the right thing and will be scored down for it.

When you do split an oversized skill, assert the split is lossless rather than trusting it: enumerate every ## section in the source and require each to land exactly once across core plus references. That makes the mechanical part of the refactor provable, and it forces the genuinely editorial parts — deduplicating a preamble that restates a section it should point at — out of the automated path where they would otherwise pass unnoticed. A skill that is vendored from upstream is a separate case: it will be overwritten on the next refresh, so leave it and note why.

A gap is often a routing problem wearing a coverage costume

Before concluding a domain is missing, check whether the vault already holds it. A collection will frequently carry a thorough skill for a subdomain that nothing points at, so the skill never wins its own search and the domain looks bare. The fix is then a pointer — a routing entry that names the skill and where to read more — rather than an acquisition, and it is the cheaper change by a wide margin.

Be careful with the tempting middle path of promoting the vault copy into the active library to make it visible. A vault skill costs zero listing tokens and is already fully reachable by search, so a copy buys no additional reach and adds a name that can collide with the original — you trade a routing problem for a hygiene one. Measure the listing saving against the collision risk before promoting anything.
